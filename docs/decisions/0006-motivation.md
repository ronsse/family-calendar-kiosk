# ADR-0006 — Duties are belonging, extras are jobs, consistency is a week

**Date:** 2026-10-22
**Status:** Accepted for §1–§6. §7 (allowance) is **Proposed**: its amount is decided (half the
child's age in dollars a week, 2026-10-22) and the rest still needs its own design review.
§8 (the grown-up app) is captured for later.
**Supersedes:** [ADR-0005](0005-streaks.md) §2, *what a streak shows*. ADR-0005 §1 (what
counts), §3 and §4 still hold, and §4 of this record builds on them.

**Context:** The household asked for streaks, routine check-boxes, and rewards for a week or a
month of consistency. They asked that every part follow current research on motivating
children rather than taste. They also set out a view on money: children owe the household
duties they can be proud of, and extra work is separate from those duties. This record is what
the research supports, and what it changes on the wall. The children are 3, 5 and 7.

---

## What the research says

| Finding | Source | What it means here |
|---|---|---|
| Rewards a child **expects** for simply doing a task lower their interest in it afterwards. The effect is stronger in children than in adults. Positive feedback raises interest. | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (128 studies). The counter-review, Cameron & Pierce 1994, agrees on this case: expected tangible rewards for doing a task. | No points or promised prizes for duties. Praise is the one reward that adds motivation. |
| Toddlers rewarded with a toy for helping later helped **less**. Toddlers who were praised did not. | Warneken & Tomasello 2008 | Children start out wanting to help, and paying for it can replace that motive. Never pay for something a child already does gladly. |
| Asking 3- to 6-year-olds to **"be a helper"** produced about 29% more helping than asking them **"to help"**. | Bryan, Master & Walton 2014. One small study. The adult version (voting) did not replicate at scale: Gerber et al. 2016, and again in 2023. | Use the noun, *helper*, when inviting a child to a duty. It costs nothing, so it stays, but it is a cheap nudge and not a proven effect. |
| Praising **how** a child did something ("you kept going") outlasts praising the **child** ("you're so smart"). After a failure, children praised for ability give up sooner. | Mueller & Dweck 1998; Kamins & Dweck 1999 | Thank a child for what they did and what it changed, not for being "the best". |
| Parents' **process praise** (effort, strategy, actions) to 1- to 3-year-olds predicted a child's appetite for challenge and belief that ability grows, five years later. Person praise did not. | Gunderson et al. 2013. Correlational: it follows 53 families and cannot prove cause. | Praise effort and persistence where there was effort. |
| Being shown a **broken** streak lowers effort, regardless of how well the person actually did. It is worse when they blame themselves, and softer when the streak can be repaired. | Silverman & Barasch 2023 | Show nothing on the wall that can break. |
| Missing **one day** did not change how fast a habit became automatic. The median was 66 days. | Lally et al. 2010 | A count that resets after one miss measures something that does not matter for habits. |
| A **Monday** or the **1st of the month** makes people start again, and past misses get filed under the "old self". | Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014 | Every week starts clean. |
| Doing chores in kindergarten predicted competence and life satisfaction in third grade. Children who take part in real family work pitch in without being asked. | ~10,000-child kindergarten cohort; Tepper 2022; Rogoff's learning by observing and pitching in | Duties are part of family life, done alongside the family, not tasks handed out to the children. |
| Keep allowance separate from chores. Allowance teaches money; chores are done because you live there. | Lieber, *The Opposite of Spoiled*. The research behind this is thin and correlational, so it is expert consensus rather than a proven result. | §7. |
| Children have the basic money concepts by about **seven**: value, exchange, earning, and putting off a want. | Whitebread & Bingham 2013 (Cambridge, for the UK Money Advice Service) | The eldest is at the age for an allowance now. |

## 1. Duties are unpaid, and the words say they are belonging

Duties stay at zero points. That was already true, and it is now a rule rather than a default.
The words around duties change in four places:

| Where | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The section heading, which is the invitation | The **noun** | "Family helpers" |
| A duty a child does alone | The **action** | "Set the table" |
| A duty a child does **with** a grown-up | "Help …", because it is true | "Help cook dinner" |
| After the child taps it | What changed for the family, and a thank-you | "The table's ready, so we can all eat. Thanks, helper." |

"Help" is not added to every title. In the study, the verb was the weaker of the two framings.
Where the child really is assisting a grown-up, "help" is accurate, so those titles use it.

The after-tap line thanks the child for an act. It does not grade the child. "You are the
best" (the dog sheet's all-done line) is person praise, and it changes to describe what was done.

**Effort or the act.** Both are *process* praise, which is what the research favours over
praise of the person. Which one fits depends on whether the thing took effort:

| What happened | Praise | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A quick tap, a two-minute duty | The act and its effect | "The table's ready, so we can all eat." |
| A whole routine, a whole week, a hard job | The effort and the persistence | "You did bedtime every night this week. You kept at it." |
| A child stuck at it, or came back to it | The strategy | "You went back for the last toy. That's how a room gets done." |
| Never | The person | "You're the best." "Such a good girl." |

Effort praise on a five-second tap rings false, and children notice. Save it for things that
took effort.

**Which word: duty, contribution, helper.** No study compares *duty* with *contribution*.
What the research has is three things:

- The **noun** framing (*helper*) has one small positive study with children and failed
  replications with adults (above). It stays as a heading, as a cheap nudge.
- Rogoff's research on families where children help without being asked calls it **pitching
  in** to a shared family effort. The children are part of the work, not given it.
- The wording the child hears matters more than the word the code uses.

So the words are split by who reads them:

| Reader | Word | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The code, schema and docs | `duty` | Exact, and already in every table. Not shown to a child. |
| Grown-ups (settings, the phone app) | "contribution", or "family jobs" | What it is: a share of the family's work. |
| The children, as the heading | "Family helpers" | A noun a 3-year-old knows, and the noun framing. |
| The children, in conversation | "We all pitch in", "in this family we help" | Belonging, in words a 3-year-old understands. |

*Duty* sounds like an obligation imposed from outside, and *contribution* is a word the
youngest does not know. Neither appears on the children's screens.

## 2. Extras are jobs, and play never pays

An extra is work beyond the daily duties: something a grown-up would otherwise do, or pay
someone to do. Effort sets the price, as it already does. Two things are **never** extras:

- **Play.** Fetch with the dog was paid 2 points. It became unpaid on 2026-10-22. It is still an
  extra, so it stays on the dog's sheet and never counts toward a full day.
- **Self-care.** Teeth, dressing and pyjamas are routine steps (§3).

## 3. Self-care goes in routines, family care in duties, and tidying happens nightly

- **Routines** hold what a child does for themselves: dressed, teeth, shoes, bag, pyjamas.
  Brushing teeth at bedtime is already a bedtime step for every child.
- **Duties** hold what a child does for the family.
- **Tidying moves from a weekly clean-up to a little every night.** A "Toys away" step opens
  the bedtime routine for every child: five minutes, in whatever room they played in. The big
  weekly clean-ups become a short weekly reset, or retire once the nightly step holds. Two
  reasons:
  - **Habit research.** Habits form from small actions repeated against a stable cue.
  - **The lesson itself.** Picking up a little as you go is easier than doing it all on one day.

  Done 2026-10-22. "Toys away" is the first bedtime step for all three children, applied through
  `provision.ts --routine-steps`, which adds a step without re-applying the household SQL. The
  Saturday basement clean-up retired the same day.

## 4. Duties attach to meals, and a full day counts only what was asked

The household's schedules vary from week to week. Meals do not: dinner happens every day,
usually between five and six. So:

- **Duties attach to the meals.** Clearing your own plate is a duty for every child after every
  meal. Setting the table, clearing it and loading the dishwasher stay a dinner rotation. A meal
  is the stable cue the habit research asks for, and it happens whatever else is going on.
- **Breakfast has no helper jobs.** A grown-up usually makes it before the children are up
  (decided 2026-10-22). Clearing your own plate still applies.
- **Walking the dog is a rotating duty, not a paid extra** (2026-10-22). Its roster order puts
  the walk on whoever clears the table that day. That leaves the dog's sheet, which shows only
  paid pet extras, and puts the walk on the child's own board.
- **Away for a job** (*built 2026-10-22*, `src/domain/chores/away.ts`). An activity between
  five and six takes a child away from dinner on some days. The rule is **deterministic, not an
  agent**: when a rotation chore with a due time is materialized, a child with a timed event
  overlapping that job's own half-hour (the 30 minutes before `due_by_local`) sits that turn
  out. The job passes to the next child in the roster, and the away child gets no row for it,
  so their day stays neutral rather than unfilled. Passing a turn does not move the rotation:
  tomorrow's assignee is unchanged. If every child is away, the plain assignee keeps the job.
  It is decided once, from calendar rows already in SQLite, never at render time; an activity
  added later the same day does not re-deal a turn already handed out.

  **Why the job's time and not the whole dinner window.** The first version of this rule sat a
  child out of every dinner job. Measured against the household's calendar (counts only), the
  two weekly activities in the dinner window both end by six and both take the two older
  children. Sitting them out of all of dinner would have handed the 3-year-old all three
  dinner jobs twice a week, though both older children are home long before the table is
  cleared (19:00) or the dishwasher loaded (19:30). Keyed to each job's time, an activity costs
  exactly the jobs it overlaps: on those days the older two miss setting the table (due 17:30)
  and nothing else.

  **Whose event it is.** Every calendar in this household is shared, so an event is a child's
  when its title names the child as a whole word (any case), or when its calendar belongs to
  that child (`calendar_source.child_id`). All-day events never count; a day off is not an
  absence. It applies to every rotation chore with a due time, so the dog walk follows the same
  rule.

  An agent is the wrong tool for a decision made every day at five. Where an agent does help is
  offline: a grown-up asks one to draft or rebalance `db/chores.json` against a month of the
  calendar, then reviews the diff and applies it with `--chores`.
- **A full day** = the morning routine (on days it runs) + that day's duties, **with one duty
  allowed to stay undone**, + the bedtime routine. Extras never count. The allowance was the
  household's call on 2026-10-24, the day the eldest got two daily duties of her own: "every
  duty, every day" made the day hardest for the child with the most asked of her. One miss is
  the same allowance the week already gives (Lally), applied a level down. At least one duty
  must be done, so a day with a single duty still needs it. `dayCounts` in
  `src/domain/chores/streaks.ts` is the one definition, and the live streak already uses it.
  ADR-0005 §1 still decides what counts as done:
  - a `claimed` duty counts, without a grown-up's approval;
  - a day with nothing asked is neutral;
  - `superseded` leaves the day;
  - an unfinished today never counts against the child.
- **A day off.** A grown-up can mark a day off for one child, for example when they are away,
  ill, or out all day. That day becomes neutral, the same as a day with nothing asked. *To
  build.*

## 5. Show the week, not a streak

Each child's screen shows **this week as seven dots, Monday to Sunday.**

- A dot fills when that day is full.
- An unfilled day is an empty dot. It is never a red mark or a zero.
- Monday starts a new week. This is the built-in fresh start.
- Nothing on the wall counts runs of days, so nothing can break.

This replaces ADR-0005 §2's "N day streak" and closes its unsolved risk: a long streak that
vanishes is itself a visible signal of failure.

The longer view **only goes up**:

- **A good week** is one with no more than one unfilled asked day (a neutral day is not
  unfilled). The one-miss allowance comes from Lally, and it is a household setting. The
  household confirmed one on 2026-10-22: a week with one missed day still counts as good.
- Each child carries a **lifetime count of good weeks**. It never decreases.
- The grown-up view also shows good weeks per month.

## 6. Celebrate after the fact, never promise in advance

- **All done.** When a child finishes a routine, or does enough of the day's duties for the
  day to count (§4: one may stay undone), the wall plays a short celebration: motion for
  everyone, plus words for the act (§1). It fires once per section a day, so finishing the last
  duty as well does not play it again. It varies, so it stays a little unpredictable. Nothing
  like this exists in `web/src` yet. `docs/calendar-structure.md` already calls the completion
  animation the youngest's reward.
- **A good week** is announced on the child's own screen. When every child has one, it is
  announced for the family as well.
- **Treats are chosen by the grown-ups, off the wall.** No prize ladder, no "7 days = a toy",
  and the wall never shows a treat in advance. A promised prize is exactly the expected,
  completion-based reward the meta-analysis found undermines motivation. An unexpected treat
  after a good week is not.
- **Praise fires on the claim, not on the approval.** Approval gates points. It never gates
  the celebration, because feedback works best when it is immediate (ADR-0005 rule 2, for the
  same reason).

## 7. Allowance (Proposed: needs a design review)

The proposal, for review:

- **A fixed weekly allowance, not tied to duties.** It is never docked for a missed duty.
  Duties are enforced the way any family expectation is, by a grown-up, not by the ledger.
- **Extras keep paying on top.** Their rates are re-checked once an allowance exists, so that
  extras stay a top-up rather than the main income. One daily shared extra at its current price
  can come to several dollars a week per child, which is more than a starting allowance.
- **Split three ways: spend, save, give.** The split happens when the money arrives, so the
  child sees it land in three places.
- **Real jars with real cash.** Use three clear jars per child, matching what the wall shows.
  Children this age learn money through coins and notes they can see grow and shrink. The wall
  is the record; the jars are what the child holds. This is common expert advice, not a
  controlled finding.
- **A savings goal** (a picture of the thing, and how far there is to go) is the piece most
  worth adding, because putting off a want is the habit Whitebread & Bingham name.
- **Amount.** Set it by what the allowance must cover, which is Lieber's test. Ask which small
  wants the grown-ups currently pay for, and which the child should now choose. A common
  starting rule of thumb is half the child's age in dollars per week. The youngest might start
  with coins for the jars and no spending. **Decided 2026-10-22: half the child's age in
  dollars a week**, which is $3.50, $2.50 and $1.50 at 7, 5 and 3. The amount is settled; the
  questions below are not.
- **Clear jars rather than piggy banks.** An opaque piggy bank hides the very thing a child this
  age is learning, which is money piling up and running down. A clear bank with three
  sections (spend, save, give) does the same job as three jars.

What the review must settle, and why it cannot be decided here:

- **"The ledger, not a bank"** (PRD) and **H2 Q2** chose one points ledger with no `paid`
  state. Three jars per child is three balances.
- **A coin jar was rejected** by an earlier panel (`balances.ts`). Re-open that decision on
  this record's evidence; do not quietly reverse it.
- **Payday mechanics.** Choose a day. Decide whether the allowance is a ledger entry the wall
  writes, or cash handed over with the wall only told about it. Decide whether savings are
  matched.

## 8. The grown-up app, captured for later

The phone admin (ROADMAP, M2) should carry the adult half of this record:

- **A reminder to approve.** A daily nudge when claims are waiting, so points never sit
  unapproved for days.
- **The why, in one line,** beside each control. For example: "Duties are unpaid because
  paying for helping can switch helping off."
- **Words for the conversation.** Short scripts for talking with a child about duties ("in
  this family we help"), about money (spend/save/give), and after a hard week (fresh start on
  Monday, no blame).
- **The consistency view.** Good weeks per child per month, and which days were not full, as
  information for the grown-ups. It is never shown to the children as a failure.
- **A treat log** (optional). What the family did to celebrate, so treats stay varied.

## Build order

1. All-done celebration, plus the after-tap words (§1, §6). Cheapest change with the
   strongest evidence.
2. Week dots, good weeks, and the full-day definition (§4, §5). Retires the streak line.
3. Helper wording on duties (§1). Copy only. The heading is "Family helpers" since 2026-10-22;
   duty titles and after-tap words are still to do.
4. ~~Unpay fetch~~ (§2). Done 2026-10-22.
5. ~~Nightly "Toys away" step~~ (§3). Done 2026-10-22. Clearing your own plate after every
   meal (§4) is still to do. Data only.
6. Day off for a child, and the away-for-a-job skip (§4). The skip is done 2026-10-22; the day
   off for a whole day is still to do and can reuse its "no row means neutral" shape.
7. Allowance design review, then build (§7).
8. The grown-up app items, when the phone admin is built (§8).

## What would change this

- **Comparison between siblings.** If the week dots lead the children to compare themselves
  with each other, draw them only on each child's own screen, and announce good weeks for the
  family only.
- **The celebration loses its effect.** If the children stop noticing it, vary it more before
  adding anything bigger.
- **A child's own verdict.** As with ADR-0005, the evidence is about children in general, and
  watching these three children outranks it.

## Candidate extras (for the household to choose from)

Jobs are grouped by the youngest age that can do them alone. "With a grown-up" means alongside
an adult, not instead of one.

Chosen 2026-10-22: **wipe the glass doors**, Fridays, first come, open to all three.

- **3 and up:** wipe the glass doors and low windows (spray bottle); match socks from the
  basket; dust low shelves; wash the bath toys; sort the shoe pile by the door.
- **5 and up:** fold the towel basket; wipe the kitchen cabinet fronts or the baseboards; pull
  weeds from one bed; clean out the car's back seat; empty the small bins into the big one.
- **7 and up, some with a grown-up:**
  - rake leaves (autumn);
  - shovel the front step (winter);
  - wash the car;
  - wipe the bathroom sink;
  - help clean the fish tank's glass.
- **One-off jobs.** A grown-up posts a one-off job with a price, using the existing New-chore
  sheet. This keeps the paid list fresh without growing the daily board.
